How we choose RCCL configurations — presentation draft

This page is a style-selection draft, not the final page. Each topic below is shown in 2–3 alternative presentation styles, labeled Option A/B/C — pick one per topic (mixing across topics is fine), and the final page will be assembled from the selections. The content is the same verified material throughout; only the presentation differs between options.

The problem, for all topics: per message size (18 of them), choose an algorithm, a protocol, and a channel count. One measurement is the median of three benchmark runs, costs roughly 30 seconds of cluster time, and reports all 18 sizes at once. The question each method answers differently: how to find the best configuration per size without measuring everything. All comparison numbers come from replays of every method on the same 15 measured datasets (5 collectives × 1/2/3 nodes), scored against the full grid as ground truth. Baseline page: approaches.html.

Topic 1 — The adaptive (racing) search: the method we use

Shared facts for all three options: the search sketches every algorithm/protocol combination at four anchor channel counts, eliminates combinations that trail everywhere, then refines the survivors per size. Across the 15 datasets it matched the grid's winner at 235 of 236 sizes (worst deviation 1.45%) using 351 measurements against the grid's 434.

Option AStep panels — the search in five frames

Running example throughout: broadcast at 3 nodes, the smallest real search space. Policy values (anchors {1, 8, 24, 48}, 15% elimination margin, 0.5% tie tolerance, 2% hill-climb hysteresis) are the real ones from adaptive_search.py; the busbw values drawn in Steps 2–3 are illustrative; the totals in Step 4 are measured.

Step 3Refinement — hill-climb, then the downward tie-walk

Per size, the surviving leader is refined on its channel ladder: climb to the peak, then step down while the speed stays within 0.5%.

  1. 1 — Hill-climb to the peak: a neighbour must beat the incumbent by >2% (the noise guard) to move the climb; nothing beats ch48 = 172, so the peak stands there.
  2. 2 — Downward tie-walk: ch40 = 171.5 sits inside the 0.5% tie band (≥ 171.14) — same speed within noise, and ties resolve to fewer channels, so the walk steps down one rung.
  3. 3 — The next rung, ch32 = 165, falls outside the band — the walk stops. Selected: RING/LL128/40.

Setting. Broadcast at 3 nodes. The search space is 3 combinations (RING/LL, RING/LL128, RING/SIMPLE) × 9 channel counts {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48} = 27 configurations. Stage 2 — eliminate combinations that trail everywhere. The elimination threshold at each size is the leader × 0.85:

  • At 64K: leader 2.6, threshold 2.6 × 0.85 = 2.21. RING/LL's best anchor is 2.0 (below); RING/SIMPLE's best is 1.4 (below).
  • At 64M: leader 172, threshold 172 × 0.85 = 146.2. RING/LL's best is 125 (below); RING/SIMPLE's best is 168 (above — it stays in).

A combination is eliminated only if it is below the threshold at every size. In this example RING/LL trails everywhere, so all 9 of its channel configurations are excluded after only its 4 anchor measurements. RING/SIMPLE survives on the strength of 64M alone — a single strong size is enough to stay in the race.

Stage 3 — per-size refinement, contenders only.

At 64K, the contenders (within 15% of the leader at that size) reduce to RING/LL128 alone. Its best anchor is ch1 = 2.6. The hill-climb tests the neighbouring channel count: ch2 returns 2.5. A new point must beat the incumbent by more than 2% (the hysteresis, a guard against measurement noise), so the climb stops immediately. Winner at 64K: RING/LL128/1.

At 64M, the contenders are RING/LL128 (172) and RING/SIMPLE (168). LL128's best anchor is ch48 = 172. The neighbour ch40 returns 171.5 — not a >2% improvement, so ch48 remains the incumbent. Then the downward tie-break walk runs: the 0.5% tie band is 172 × 0.995 = 171.14; ch40 at 171.5 falls inside the band, so it counts as a tie, and ties resolve to the fewest channels — the walk steps down. The next step, ch32, returns 165 — outside the band — and the walk stops. Winner at 64M: RING/LL128/40.

The cost, measured. 12 anchor measurements plus a handful of refinement steps. On the real broadcast-3n grid, the search measured 24 of the 27 configurations (72 benchmark runs against the grid's 81) and matched the grid's winner at 18 of 18 sizes. The three configurations it never touched (RING/LL/4, RING/LL/40, RING/SIMPLE/4) were never on any contender's refinement path at any size.

Why the ch=1 anchor is load-bearing. Broadcast and reduce multi-node channel curves are bimodal: ch=1 wins at 32K–512K while high channel counts win elsewhere. With anchors {8, 24, 48} the climb starts at ch8 and never looks below it, missing the ch=1 winners by up to 53% (measured 2026-08-18). Extending the anchor set to {1, 8, 24, 48} fixed exactly this.

Topic 2 — Why Optuna (Bayesian search) does not fit this problem

Shared facts: Optuna's TPE sampler was replayed on all 15 datasets under the same rules as every other method. It optimizes one scalar score per trial, has no completion signal, and re-proposes configurations it has already tried. Its median cost barely beat a random shuffle, and on 11 of the 15 datasets at least one seed in ten never found the full winner set.

Option AAssumption-by-assumption table with commentary

Optuna's operating assumptionthis problem's reality
the space is too large to enumerate (thousands of combinations or more) 20–54 candidates per grid — small enough to enumerate outright
there is one objective to maximize 18 independent per-size winners; averaging them into one score blurs exactly the distinctions being tuned
the budget is the stopping rule (“best found in N trials” is success) the requirement is the winner at every size; TPE emits no signal that says “you now have them all”
each trial proposes a fresh candidate real trace (seed 2, broadcast 3n): 810 proposals, of which 788 were repeats of configurations already tried

The single-scalar problem. TPE is handed one number per trial — here, a normalized average across the 18 sizes. But the true winners are scattered across the space: RING/LL/24 wins at 4K, RING/LL128/1 at 32K–512K, RING/LL128/48 at the large sizes. A configuration that averages well can be the winner nowhere, and a per-size winner can average poorly; the scalar erases the very structure the search must recover.

The stopping problem. In the replays, Optuna's cost is counted at the moment its evaluated set first contains every per-size winner — a check performed against the grid truth, which a real deployment does not have. Its numbers are therefore its oracle best case. Even so, its median cost when it succeeds is about 20 of 27 candidates, and on 11 of the 15 datasets at least one seed in ten never succeeded at all — on all_reduce 1n, seven of ten never did.

The duplicate behavior. TPE has no built-in de-duplication on small categorical spaces; it keeps re-proposing configurations it already believes are good. Seed 1 on broadcast 3n spent its entire 810-proposal budget (capped at 27 × 30) without ever proposing RING/LL/24 — the winner at 4K. Without that configuration its winner set could never match, at any budget. Duplicates were answered from cache and cost nothing in the replay, which again favors Optuna; it still lost.

Where Optuna is the right tool. Huge spaces (thousands of combinations or more), expensive trials, no ground truth, and success defined as “best found within a fixed budget.” If this space ever grows that large — algorithm × protocol × channels × chunk-size and threshold parameters — the assessment should be revisited. Under a relaxed success bar (within 5% of the winner) Optuna does converge in nearly all seeds at about half the cost, but the stopping problem remains — and under the same relaxation the adaptive search also gets cheaper (25–45% fewer runs at a worst-case 2.7% gap), so the comparison does not change.

Topic 3 — Dip triage: a sound physical test with a blind spot

Shared facts: bus bandwidth must rise (or flatten) as message size grows, because larger payloads amortize fixed overheads. A drop in the default configuration's size curve is therefore proof of mistuning at that size, obtained for a single measurement. The converse does not hold: a smooth curve is not proof that the default is good.

Option AThe physics argument in prose, with the measured example

The test. Measure the default configuration once (one measurement, i.e. the median of three runs) and scan its 18-point size curve. Wherever the curve drops below 90% of its running maximum, the default is provably mistuned at that size — no comparison configuration needed, because physics says the curve may only rise.

The measured example — broadcast 3n (2026-08-18). The relevant portion of the default curve reads: 32K: 0.79, 64K: 0.70, 128K: 1.55 GB/s, and the running maximum before 64K is 0.88. The detector rule at 10%: 0.70 < 0.88 × 0.9 = 0.79, so 64K is flagged — a 20.5% dip. No other size violates the rule, so the flag set is exactly {64K}. Running the adaptive search restricted to that one size costs 14 measurements in total (1 for the default curve + 13 for the search) instead of 24 for the search over all sizes, and the 64K fix it finds is the same one the full grid finds.

The blind spot, same dataset. At 128K — the size immediately next to the dip — the default value of 1.55 obeys the running-maximum rule, so 128K is not flagged. Yet the grid winner at 128K is 59% faster than the default. The same holds at 256K (51% faster) and 512K (28% faster). The default there is uniformly mediocre: slow at every neighbouring size in similar proportion, so the curve's shape reveals nothing. Measured across all 15 datasets, searching only triage-flagged sizes leaves up to 59% of available performance unrealized on broadcast 3n and 58% on reduce 2n.

The asymmetry in one sentence: a dip proves the default is mistuned; a smooth curve does not prove it is well tuned.

Topic 4 — The overall flow: where each piece lives

Shared facts: the adaptive search runs at every tuning session; the full grid is kept only as an occasional calibration that verifies the searcher; every candidate rule must pass a live A/B gate before deployment; dip triage serves as the post-deployment monitor. Optuna and random search appear nowhere in the flow — both were evaluated for the search stage and not adopted.

Option APipeline diagram with neutral labels

The full grid sits outside the routine path: it is re-run only on new hardware or a new RCCL version, to confirm the adaptive search still finds the same winners. Optuna and random search were candidates for the search box and were not adopted (Topic 2); dip triage was a candidate pre-filter and serves instead as the monitor (Topic 3). Nothing reaches deployment without passing the A/B gate: reproducible superiority over the RCCL default through the real deployment path, P(sup) ≥ 0.95 over 9 interleaved pairs per arm.

Option BOne real tuning run, start to finish

The all_reduce pilot of 2026-08-24, nodes {5,8}, jobs 20723 and 20725 — a single command: rccl-tune run --collectives all_reduce --scales 1,2 --repeats 9.

  1. Book. The node allocation is taken once and held for the whole session; every later step runs inside it.
  2. Search. The adaptive search covers the 1-node space in 72 runs against the full grid's 81 (−11%), measuring 24 configurations in 23 minutes. The 2-node space follows: 117 runs against 162 (−28%), 39 configurations, 36 minutes.
  3. Configuration. The 1-node search emits 18 candidate rules — one per message size, each naming algorithm, protocol, and channel count.
  4. A/B validation. Each rule is compared against the RCCL default through the real deployment path (the tuner plugin), 9 interleaved repeats per arm. A rule ships only with P(sup) ≥ 0.95.
  5. Verdicts. Of the 18 rules, 9 shipped — gains from +12.5% (4K) to +66.3% (512K), every one at P(sup) = 1.00 — and 9 were removed: three with no measurable gain (−0.3% to −1.9%) and six with real regressions, from −6.9% down to −65.8%.
  6. Ship. The surviving 7-line configuration (adjacent kept sizes merge) covers 4K–1M; every other size keeps the RCCL default.

What the gate caught. Two of the removed rules regressed moderately (16M at −7.4%, 32M at −6.9%). The four severe regressions at 64M–512M (−63.5% to −65.8%) share one mechanism: at those sizes the default engages an acceleration path (WarpSpeed) that runs 222–224 effective channels, while the searched rule pinned 48 — suppressing that path in deployment even though the rule had measured as a small win in the search environment. No search method, however accurate, replaces the gate: the search environment and the deployment path are demonstrably not equivalent.

The 2-node half of the same run makes the complementary point: only 2 of 18 rules survived (32M at +7.2%, 128M at +2.4%), and a 256K rule that had won the previous day did not reproduce (P(sup) 0.89) and correctly did not ship. Most of the 2-node space is already well tuned by the default — the gate is what keeps the configuration honest about that. Full detail: the live pilot.
